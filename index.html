Clutch replacement on a 2015 Mazda 3, done in my driveway

Vehicle2015 Mazda 3 hatchback, manual
Bought for$2,500 (Marketplace)
WorkspaceHome driveway
StatusRoad-ready
Complaint

The clutch failed completely on the drive home from Mississauga, on the day I bought the car. The engine ran but no power reached the wheels.

Cause

Worn-out clutch friction disc. The previous owner's wear finished it off on the first long drive.

Correction

Removed the transmission, replaced the clutch disc and pressure plate with genuine Mazda parts, reinstalled and road-tested.

Why I took it on

After doing brake and strut jobs, I wanted a bigger test of my skills. My partner also needed a car for commuting, so I looked for a Mazda 3 at a good price that I could fix myself.

How it went

  1. Bought the car and finished the paperwork

    Negotiated $2,500 on Facebook Marketplace and picked it up the following Monday.

  2. Breakdown on a busy road

    The clutch gave out near Dundas St W and Highway 427 during the afternoon. I kept traffic moving safely around the car and, with help from a passerby, pushed it out of the live lane.

  3. Arranged a tow

    Called a tow through my insurance right away and waited about 2.5 hours for the truck to reach me.

  4. Diagnosed and planned

    Confirmed the clutch had failed, then researched the procedure, made a parts list and set up a safe work area before starting.

  5. Teardown

    Supported the car on jack stands with wooden cribbing as backup, dropped the front subframe and lowered the transmission on a floor jack.

  6. New clutch installed

    Cleaned the bell housing and input shaft, then fitted the new genuine Mazda clutch disc and pressure plate.

  7. Transmission wouldn't line up

    Two neighbours helped me try to push it back into place, but the alignment was off. One of them suggested using rope to lift it instead.

  8. Built a lifting rig

    Hung a rope and pulley system from a 2x4 beam across the engine bay, which gave me enough control to bring the transmission into line and bolt it up.

  9. Road test passed

    The car now drives normally. I'm finishing cosmetic work.

The problem I didn't plan for

A transmission is heavy and has to slide onto the input shaft at exactly the right angle. Pushing from below with a floor jack and two extra people still wasn't precise enough.

A neighbour's suggestion to use rope led me to learn about mechanical advantage. I built a temporary block-and-tackle from a 2x4 beam, rope and pulleys. The pulleys reduced the force I needed and let me make small, controlled adjustments from above while the jack carried weight from below.

It was a cheap fix built from what I had, and it worked.

How I approach a problem I haven't solved before

  1. DiagnoseFind out what is actually wrong before buying anything.
  2. Analyze and planBreak the job into steps and spot what could go wrong.
  3. Gather informationResearch the procedure, specs and the tools it needs.
  4. Learn the skillPick up the new technique or knowledge the job calls for.
  5. Prepare materialsHave the parts, tools and safe supports ready before starting.
  6. Ask for help when neededBring in other people's experience when I hit a limit.

Electrical diagnosis

Tracing a dead rear wiper motor to its root cause

Vehicle2015 Mazda 3 hatchback
ComponentRear wiper motor (brushed DC)
ToolsDigital multimeter
StatusWaiting for new brushes
Complaint

The rear window wiper stopped working.

Cause

The output shaft that drives the wiper arm was seized by old grease mixed with road salt, dirt and moisture. With the shaft locked, the motor stalled every time the wiper was switched on, and the stall current burned out its carbon brushes.

Correction

Cleaned every component and re-greased the shaft so it turns freely. New brushes are on order. I'll fit them and test the motor when they arrive.

How I found the fault

I took the motor apart and tested each part of the circuit with my multimeter. Inside is a small brushed DC motor driving a gear train that turns the shaft the wiper arm mounts on.

Armature windings and other components0.2–0.5 ΩContinuity
Across the brushes and terminalsOLOpen circuit

Every part read normal except the brushes and terminals. The meter showed OL there, meaning there was no path for current to reach the armature. That pinned the fault to the brushes.

The output shaft was also stuck solid. Years of grease had mixed with road salt, dust and moisture and hardened around it.

Why a seized shaft burns out the brushes

A spinning DC motor also works as a generator. As it turns, it produces a voltage called back-EMF that pushes against the supply and limits how much current the motor draws.

When the shaft is seized, the motor can't turn, so there is no back-EMF. Current climbs to its maximum, limited only by the low resistance of the windings. My readings show why that matters: with only a fraction of an ohm in the circuit, Ohm's law gives 12 V ÷ 0.5 Ω = 24 A, far more than a small wiper motor is built to carry. That heat, plus arcing where the brushes meet the commutator, is what burned them.

New brushes alone would have burned out again, so I fixed the seized shaft first.

What this project shows

  • Using a meter to diagnoseTested the circuit before replacing parts.
  • Motor theory in practiceLinked a mechanical fault to an electrical failure.
  • Root-cause repairFixed what killed the brushes, not just the brushes.
